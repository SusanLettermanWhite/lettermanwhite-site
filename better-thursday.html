Readiness for Change

A Better Thursday

By Susan Letterman White

On a Thursday in March, two people on the team went home at five. Nobody said a word. Everybody noticed.

1The familiar Thursday

Every Thursday looks the same.

Twelve people. One shared drive. Thursday is report day, and the report takes most of the day.

They have worked this way for years. It works well enough. Everyone knows where everything is. Everyone knows the rhythm of the week.

Familiar feels comfortable.

2The announcement

A different Thursday nobody asked for.

Leadership licenses an AI tool and says, "See what it can do." Training sessions fill the calendar.

Three people attend. The rest have reasons. They are too busy. They are not sure the tool is accurate. They are not sure what it means for their jobs.

Nobody dislikes Thursday. It is long, but it is theirs. To the team, a different Thursday is the risk.

The invitation stands. People keep their Thursday.

3The step that gets skipped

Change starts when the present feels wrong.

Before people embrace something new, they have to experience the present as limiting. They have to feel unhappy with the way things are.

That feeling is hard to create. People feel comfortable with what they know. They fear and avoid what they don't.

Most change efforts skip this step. They describe the future, announce a date, and wait for people to move.

You cannot order people to dislike their Thursday.

4The phone in the pocket

Nobody hated their flip phone.

Think back to the first iPhones. Most people felt nothing wrong with the phone they had. It made calls. It sent texts. It was fine.

Then a friend had something different. Then a few more. Then a lot of people did.

The discomfort never came from the old phone. It came from the difference. People did not like feeling inadequate, or less than.

Other people's days had changed. Theirs had not.

The gap did what no announcement could.

5The gap opens

Two colleagues leave at five.

Dana and Luis were two of the three who went to training. Now they draft the weekly report in an hour. They spend the rest of Thursday on the hard questions their director keeps asking. Then they go home.

Nobody announces it. People notice.

The afternoon begins to feel long. The shared drive begins to feel slow. The old rhythm sounds a little off.

Thursday feels longer than it used to.

6Why this works for AI

Some changes spread person to person.

A restructuring arrives all at once. Everyone moves on the same day. Comparison plays little part.

A developmental change works the way the phone did. A few people go first. Others feel the difference. Then they choose.

Learning to work with AI is that kind of change. The early adopters are the nudge.

7Finding Dana and Luis

Leaders do not have to wait for luck.

Dana and Luis started on their own. That was luck.

Every organization has two maps. The org chart shows who reports to whom. The informal map shows relationships: who people go to for advice, whose judgment they trust, and whose opinions, likes, and dislikes travel.

Organizational network analysis draws the second map. It shows the people others already watch.

Bring those people in first. Give them the tool, the time, and a voice in the story. Adoption becomes faster and more likely when the influential early adopters are on board first.

From my own work

In a recent restructuring, the change was structural: from silos to teams. We mapped the working relationships of 19 senior leaders across seven dimensions. The map showed whom the others already turned to, and so where to begin.

8A story worth moving toward

Then someone describes a better Thursday.

Discomfort makes people want to leave. It does not tell them where to go.

The director tells a story. A year from now, the first draft of the report is ready by noon. The team spends its afternoons on the questions that need human judgment. Each person decides which part of the work they want back.

She tells it often. She tells it to the skeptics first. She ties it to why the team exists.

Now the team can picture next Thursday. The old rhythm is still there, in a new key.

9The Thursdays in between

Wanting a better Thursday is not enough.

The team also needs time and skill. The director protects two hours every Thursday for practice. Dana and Luis teach the others. The policies catch up with the work.

That is readiness. It has two halves.

Logistics: what existsPeople, time, budget, space, technology, information, skills, belief in their ability to learn, and a culture that supports the change
Psychology: what people feel and believeDislike of the present, desire for a clearly defined future that is clearly better, and confidence that they can learn something new

When both are in place, Thursday changes week by week, for everyone.

Readiness for Change

Thursday was comfortable.

That was always the obstacle.

Change does not start with the future. It starts with how the present feels. Then it needs people who go first, a story worth moving toward, and the time and skill to follow.

Most organizational change efforts begin with the announcement. This team began to move when Thursday became an uncomfortable reminder that some colleagues were finishing their reports and leaving work earlier, and those left behind wanted to be included. It kept moving because people could picture a better one.

What had been familiar no longer felt comfortable. The change that had felt risky now felt valuable and worth their time.

On a Thursday in June, twelve people went home at five.

The next week, the team down the hall noticed.
